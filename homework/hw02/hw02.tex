\hwsection{Section A \textperiodcentered\ Mathematics 1.2 --- Multiplying and Dividing Integers}

\begin{remember}
  Dividing \key{undoes} multiplying, so \key{$\times$ and $\div$ obey the same four
  rules}:

  \medskip
  \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
    \key{Same} signs      & the answer is \key{positive} \quad $-6\times-7=42$ \quad $-42\div-7=6$ \\
    \key{Different} signs & the answer is \key{negative} \quad $-6\times7=-42$ \quad $-42\div7=-6$ \\
  \end{tabularx}

  \medskip
  The \key{product} of two numbers is the answer when you \key{multiply} them. The
  product of $2$ and $-9$ is $-18$.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Careful.} ``Two negatives make a positive'' is only true for
  \key{$\times$ and $\div$}. For \key{adding} it is false: $-3+-4=-7$, because adding
  a negative still sends you \key{left}.
\end{remember}

(v) $0$ \quad (vi) $-18$

\smallskip
\noindent (b) They have used the $\times$ and $\div$ rule inside a bracket that
contains an \textbf{addition}. Two negatives only make a positive when you multiply or
divide, so $-3+-5=-8$, not $8$, and the answer is $-32$.

\smallskip
\noindent{\small\textbf{This is the question of the sheet.} Expect a large part of the
class to get (b) wrong, or to write ``they added instead of multiplying''. That is
worth ten minutes of whole-class discussion rather than a red pen: the misconception is
the sentence itself, not the sum. Ask them to say out loud which \emph{operation} the
rule belongs to.}

\qhead[cbred]{A5}{Catch it with an estimate}
\noindent (a) $-5\times20=-100$, so the answer should be about $-100$.

\smallskip
\noindent (b) His answer is \emph{positive}, so he has forgotten the sign: a negative
times a positive is negative. The digits were fine --- the true answer is $-102.9$.

\smallskip
\noindent{\small Accept any sensible rounding: $-5\times21=-105$ is just as good. Do
not mark the estimate against the exact answer --- mark whether it was close enough to
catch the error. That is the only reason estimating is on this sheet, and it is why the
four rounding drills that would normally sit here are not: Workbook Exercise 1.2 Q8 and
Q9 already set exactly those, so repeating them here would waste the homework.}

\qhead[cbred]{A6}{Word problems}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., itemsep=3pt]
  \item (a) $6\times-15=-90$ points. \; (b) $-120\div-15=8$ questions. \\
        {\small Part (b) is the only place on this sheet where a negative divided by a
        negative appears in a real context. The answer is a \emph{count}, so it has to
        come out positive --- that is a good sanity check to point out.}
  \item (a) $8\times-7=-56$ dollars. \; (b) $-8\times-7=+56$, so his total changes by
        $+56$ dollars. \; (c) Better off, by 56 dollars --- he is back to zero. \\
        {\small This is the honest real-world meaning of ``negative times negative'':
        taking away 8 debts. If a student writes $8\times-7$ for (b) and gets $-56$
        again, ask them whether cancelling a debt makes you richer or poorer. Most will
        answer that correctly out loud and then see their own sign error.}
  \item (a) $10\times4=40$. \; (b) $10\times-4=-40$. \; (c) $40+-40=0$. \\
        {\small The deadpan one. Mr Bowen has scored zero on his own quiz and is very
        pleased with himself. Part (c) is an \emph{addition}, so this is one more place
        the false version of ``two negatives make a positive'' can be caught.}
\end{enumerate}

\qhead[cbred]{A7}{Now you write the question}
\noindent There is no single right answer. Check the four conditions in the green box,
in this order:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Is it a repeated lot, or a sharing out?} (a) must be \emph{five lots of
        $-8$} --- five hours, five people, five wrong answers. (b) must be a total of
        $-60$ \emph{split} into 12 equal parts. Turning (b) into a multiplication is
        the most common miss.
  \item \textbf{Does the signal word match?} (a) needs \eng{each}, \eng{every} or
        \eng{per}. (b) needs \eng{shared between}, \eng{divided by} or an equal rate.
  \item \textbf{Full sentences, ending in a question?}
  \item \textbf{Does their own answer come out as $-40$, and as $-5$?}
\end{enumerate}

\smallskip
\noindent{\small Two that work: (a) ``Mr Bowen loses 8 dollars every day for 5 days.
What is the change to his money?'' (b) ``A diver goes down 60 metres in 12 minutes at a
steady rate. What is the change in her depth each minute?'' \\
Expect (b) to be harder than (a) by a distance, because a division story needs a
\emph{rate} and most of them will write a subtraction instead. Discuss it as a class
before marking it.}

\qhead[cbred]{B1}{The words for comparing two things}
\noindent 1.\ Both \ldots\ and \quad 2.\ but \quad 3.\ Unlike \quad 4.\ have \quad
5.\ similar \ldots\ the same

\smallskip
\noindent{\small Sentence 4 catches the students who write ``has''. ``Both kinds of
cell'' is plural, so the verb is \emph{have}. Worth saying once, clearly.}

\qhead[cbred]{B2}{True or false?}
\noindent 1.\ True \quad 2.\ \textbf{False} \quad 3.\ True \quad 4.\ True \quad
5.\ True \quad 6.\ \textbf{False}

\smallskip
\noindent Rewrites: \; 2.\ ``An animal cell has \emph{no} cell wall.'' (Or: ``A
\emph{plant} cell has a cell wall made of cellulose.'') \; 6.\ ``Chloroplasts are green
because they contain \emph{chlorophyll}.''

\smallskip
\noindent{\small Sentence 6 is a vocabulary trap, not a biology one: \emph{cellulose}
and \emph{chlorophyll} both begin with c-h/c-e and both belong to plants. If the class
mixes them up, the fix is that cellulose is the \emph{building material of the wall}
and chlorophyll is the \emph{green colour that catches sunlight}.}

\qhead[cbred]{B3}{Answer in full sentences}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., itemsep=3pt]
  \item Every animal cell has a cell membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria and a nucleus.
  \item An animal cell has no fixed shape because it has no cell wall to hold it
        stiff, and an animal has to bend and move.
  \item Any two, each a full sentence. For example: ``An animal cell has no cell wall,
        but a plant cell has one.'' ``Unlike a plant cell, an animal cell has no
        chloroplasts, so it cannot make its own food.'' ``A plant cell has a large sap
        vacuole, but an animal cell does not.''
\end{enumerate}
\noindent{\small Mark the \emph{sentence}, not just the fact. A list of three words is
not an answer to any of these, and saying so consistently is the only thing that
changes it.}

\qhead[cbred]{B4}{Two pictures down a microscope}
\noindent (a) Picture 1 shows plant cells, because the cells have straight, stiff edges
and are packed together like bricks --- that is a cell wall, and only plants have one.
Picture 2 shows animal cells, because the cells are soft and rounded with no straight
edges, which means there is no cell wall.

\smallskip
\noindent (b) The student is wrong because not every plant cell has chloroplasts: an
onion bulb grows underground in the dark, so its cells are not green, and they are still
plant cells. Green is a clue that can fail; the \emph{cell wall} is the clue that never
does.

\smallskip
\noindent{\small (b) is the hardest question in Section B and the one most worth
discussing. ``No green means animal'' is the misconception the class arrived at in the
lesson, and it needs breaking twice before it goes.}

4 --- mitochondrion

\smallskip
